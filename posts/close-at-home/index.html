I Learned the Month-End Close at Work. Then I Did It at Home, Because I Have No Hobbies.

I asked my AI CFO if I could buy a video game. It did a variance analysis on my fun.

September 26, 2026 · 5 min read · Also on Medium

I asked my CFO if I could buy Civilization.

It checked my cash against my safety floor. It looked at what I'd already spent on games this cycle. It worked out that the purchase would eat about 14% of my monthly surplus. It laid out two timing options. And it finished with: "I'd wait for a sale. Civ games are discounted often."

I have been financially advised by my own software to wait for a Steam sale. I've never felt so seen.

My CFO is an AI agent running in Claude Code. I'm the CEO of a company called Home Inc, which is my household. Headcount: me, and a few agents who never ask for a raise.

I didn't know what a "close" was

I work at Ledge, where AI agents prepare the month-end close for finance teams: the reconciliations, the flux analysis, the working papers. The humans review and approve, instead of rebuilding everything from scratch every month. Their line is "The close, prepared."

Before that, my financial strategy was a budgeting app: categorize every transaction, balance every envelope, feel guilty about a falafel.

Then I learned what real companies do. They don't chase every falafel. They close the month:

  1. Reconcile. Make sure the books match reality.
  2. Flux. Compare this period to the last one. Ignore what's stable. Look hard only at what moved.
  3. Explain the variances. Every big movement gets a reason, or a question.
  4. Sign off. Someone accountable says: this month is closed.
  5. Look forward. What does this mean for next month?

And I had the thought that ruins your evenings: my household has income, expenses, obligations and goals. Why am I running it like a hobby?

The hour that helped nobody

For years I used YNAB. Great tool. I was a disciplined user, which is a polite way of saying I was a man in a spreadsheet at 11 p.m.

Even with AI helping, it took about an hour every month.

And after that hour, I still couldn't answer the only question that mattered: can I afford this?

I had perfect records of the past and zero help with the decision in front of me. It was like a history museum with no exit.

The CEO doesn't reconcile

So I stopped budgeting and started acting like a company.

  • I became the CEO. My job: set goals and constraints. A safety margin. A target monthly surplus. What we're saving for.
  • An AI agent became the CFO. It reads the accounts (read-only, it can't touch a shekel) and takes care of the day to day.

And the best part: I stopped caring about individual transactions. Unless a transaction explains a variance, it can live its life in peace. I traded resolution for signal.

My CFO asks three questions:

  1. Is the safety margin intact?
  2. Is the surplus in its normal range?
  3. Is there a movement I can't explain?

Yes, yes, no? Close it. Go outside.

Then I made it continuous

Ledge's insight is that a close shouldn't be a monthly panic. Reconciliation should run in the background, and people should see only the exceptions.

So I gave my house the same deal. The CFO doesn't wait for the end of the month to find out something is off. It reconciles as things happen, compares against the baseline, and stays quiet when everything is boring.

When nothing's wrong, I hear nothing. When something is, I get one line: what moved, why, and whether it matters.

For a household, this is the luxury tier of finance. My books close themselves, and my only job is to be surprised responsibly.

What an actual answer looks like

Back to Civ. The CFO's reply, condensed:

Yes, you can buy it. It's a fine one-off, but it goes over the games budget.

  • Cash: well above your safety floor, even after the card charge.
  • Games budget: a small allowance per cycle that accumulates. Nothing is saved up yet.
  • Effect: the price equals about two cycles of games budget, so it comes out of your normal surplus, roughly 14% of it.

Option 1: buy now, and it counts as ordinary spending. Option 2: buy after the new cycle starts, and half the price is covered by allowance.

One check first: confirm the price. I'd wait for a sale.

Three things I love:

  • It thinks in my goals, not in shekels. "14% of your surplus" is a sentence with feelings in it.
  • It gives options, not orders. I can overrule it. But now I overrule it with data, like a professional.
  • It says what it didn't check. It told me it hadn't compared the purchase against a bigger goal we're saving for, and that it would at the next close. My CFO shows more humility than most humans I've worked with, and I include myself.

The setup (it's almost embarrassingly simple)

Home Inc is Claude Code and a folder full of Markdown files. Each agent has its own subfolder, its own rules, and its own notes.

Here's the part people don't expect: I never open those files. How the agents organize their notes, what they name things, where they keep the minutes, is their problem. I'm the CEO. I don't read the CFO's filing system, I read the answer.

If a company's CEO had to browse the shared drive to trust the finance team, that company has a bigger problem.

House rules for my AI employees

Home Inc has access to my money and my mail, so I assume the world is hostile (I come from application security, so this is less a choice and more a condition). The staff handbook:

  • Nobody acts. Agents draft and advise. I decide, and I press the button.
  • Nobody moves money. Read-only access.
  • Nobody invents numbers. If it isn't in the books, the answer is "unknown." This alone beats several people I've met.
  • Everything from outside is suspicious. Anything that arrives from the world meets the least powerful part of the system first. (For the OWASP crowd: yes, it's a trust boundary. No, I won't draw you the diagram.)

Steal this

You don't need agents to run a close at home:

  1. Write down 3 numbers: your safety margin, your normal monthly surplus, and your goals.
  2. Once a month, ask "what changed?" instead of "where did it go?"
  3. Ask for a verdict, not a report. Pick who answers "can I afford this?" and let them say no.

The last one is the hardest, and the one that matters. A finance function that only says yes isn't a finance function. It's an enabler with a login.

I got back an hour a month. More importantly, I became the person who decides, not the person who reconciles. And on the weekend, I can go back to conquering the world, budget-approved, in a game about conquering the world.

Next: an HR office that reviews the other offices. I'm told this is how it starts.
